How to review

  • Pick a screen from the selector in the dark bar, or step with the arrows (Alt + ← / → on a keyboard). The sidebar menu works too, and "+ New" opens the create menu.
  • Design notes opens a panel per screen: what was kept, what is new (with the feature number), what moved. Small copper numbers on the page mark the spots the notes talk about.
  • Click things. Tickets open side sheets, status buttons move the status track, chips toggle, segmented controls switch views, and Save buttons confirm with a toast. Nothing is saved anywhere.
  • Read the three reports in this Review group: the design system, the feature research behind the twenty additions, and the architecture review of the current code.

What stayed the same

  • The palette, the IBM Plex type, and the Fieldline wordmark.
  • Every destination the office has today, the same three big tools for techs (Timesheet, Lookup, My vehicle), and the company and signed-in-as switchers.
  • The component kinds: pill buttons, chips, segmented controls, tinted job tickets, the dark queue rail on the board, fact grids, and the one shared Back bar with Save and Save and back.
  • Every capability on every current screen. The inventory in the research folder was the checklist.

What changed everywhere

  • A menu instead of a tab row. Eighteen wrapped tabs became a sidebar grouped the way the office thinks: Work, Customers, Sales, Money, Insights, with Users and Settings at the bottom. It collapses to icons. Creating anything lives in one "+ New" button in the top bar, and the follow-up count sits on the bell. The new areas are marked new in the menu.
  • Filters behind a button. Chip walls are gone from every page; a "Filters · 2" button opens a sheet with the same choices, and the active count stays visible.
  • One layout grammar. A toolbar with one row of controls and a quiet summary line, a work list on the left, the canvas in the middle, and a side sheet for the record you clicked. Modal windows are gone; the board stays visible.
  • One color language for job state. Steel is scheduled, amber is on the way, moss is on site or done, rust is waiting on parts, red is urgent or late, plum is a quoted job. It is the same on tickets, pills, board slots, map pins, and the phone.
  • Summary before detail. Counts, money, and what is late sit at the top of every page, so nobody has to count cards or scroll to know the state of the day.
  • Motion with a job. Lists rise in, the status track and pills animate on change, sheets slide, meters fill, charts draw. Everything respects reduced-motion settings.
  • Bigger targets on the phone. Status buttons and the Complete flow are thumb sized; the command box from the README ("Close, 1.5 hrs, filter change") gets a real home at the bottom of the tech's day with a parsed preview.

Design system · Fieldline

Paper, ink, copper

The system behind every screen in this pass. It keeps Fieldline's palette and type and turns them into tokens, a small set of components, one layout grammar, and rules for state, numbers and motion. The CSS in design/redesign-2026-10/fieldline-ds.css is its first implementation and is meant to become the product's stylesheet.

Layout grammar

Office
  1. Sidebar menu: grouped Work, Customers, Sales, Money, Insights; Users and Settings at the bottom; collapses to icons. The top bar holds the page title, the follow-up bell, and the one "+ New" button.
  2. Toolbar: one row at most: a segmented control, a date or search, a Filters button, one or two buttons, and a quiet summary line.
  3. Rail: the work list on the left: a search, a segmented control, a Filters button, the list.
  4. Canvas: the board, calendar, table or record.
  5. Sheet: the record you clicked slides in from the right; the page stays put. "Open full" goes to the record page.
  6. Back bar on every record and editor: "Back to where I came from" left, Save and Save and back right, a discard prompt if dirty.
Phone
  1. One task per screen, the next action as the biggest button.
  2. Status track at the top of a job, status buttons under it.
  3. Sections as rows with counts; bottom sheets for detail.
  4. Sync pill always visible; nothing blocks on the network.
  5. Command box at the bottom of the day: type or speak "Close, 1.5 hrs, filter change" and see it parsed before it applies.

Motion

Durations
120 ms hover and press · 220 ms state change · 380 ms screens and sheets
Easing
Standard for state, an expo-out for entrances, a small spring only on toggles and checkmarks
Patterns
Screens rise in; lists stagger by 40 ms; sheets slide; meters and bars grow from their baseline; lines draw; live dots pulse; skeletons shimmer while syncing
Restraint
One orchestrated moment per screen. Nothing loops except the live pulse. prefers-reduced-motion turns it all off.

Rules

  • Summary before detail. Counts, money and lateness at the top of every page.
  • State in form, not only in words. A pill or tint for every state; color never alone (the word is always there).
  • Numbers in mono, aligned. Tabular figures in every column; money with cents only on documents.
  • Copper is scarce. One copper button per screen, the "now" line, the selected option. Everything else is ink or ghost.
  • Prefill and say so. Anything the app already knows is filled and tagged; nobody picks the same thing twice.
  • Tap targets of 44 px on the phone. Status buttons are 56 px.
  • Charts use one hue. Copper for the measure, a neutral for de-emphasis, a darker copper ramp for ordered categories; direct labels on the ends; a hover value on every mark. Steel and moss are too close for side-by-side series.
  • Empty states say what will appear and how to add it. Never a blank panel.
  • Night mode is possible, not designed. Every color is a token, so a dark theme for techs working after dark is a later, deliberate pass, not an inversion.

Handoff

Tokens are CSS custom properties on :root; components are plain class names with no framework dependency, so they port to CSS Modules or Tailwind v4 unchanged. The next step in the build is a Storybook of these components taken from this stylesheet, then the screens assembled from them.

Research · October 2026

What HVAC dispatch software has, and what Fieldline should add

Sixteen platforms were surveyed, from ServiceTitan and Housecall Pro to BuildOps and Simpro, alongside G2, Capterra and Trustpilot reviews, HVAC trade press, the EPA and CARB refrigerant rules, and California's Title 24 process. Thirty candidate features came out. Twenty made the cut for a 2–10 technician commercial and light-commercial shop in Orange County; ten were left out on purpose.

The full research notes, with about 140 source links, are in the repo under design/redesign-2026-10/research/: the platform feature landscape, the pain points and 2025–26 trends, the current UI inventory, the data model read, and the reference architectures.

Where Fieldline already stands

The current build is further along than its README admits. It already has what the market treats as table stakes for the core loop: a drag-and-drop day board with helpers and return visits, a follow-up queue with wait reasons, a tech status flow with signature capture, California-aware timesheets with meal and rest tracking, quotes with twenty HVAC scope templates and margin pricing, purchase orders with partial receiving, truck stock by dollar category, and billing for T&M, not-to-exceed, quoted and maintenance work with revisions and credits. The gaps are the layers around that loop: nothing talks to the customer, nothing talks to the field in real time, nothing remembers the equipment well enough, and nothing collects money.

The twenty, ranked

Ranked by expected value to this company, with a complexity size (S, M, L) and the evidence that put it on the list.

F1 · size M

Live map and GPS tech tracking

Every one of the sixteen platforms puts a map with live tech positions next to the board. Dispatchers say the alternative is a phone call to ask where someone is.

Needs the tech app to report location; phone GPS is enough to start, hardware telematics later.
F2 · size M

Capacity view and assignment suggestions

ServiceTitan's Adaptive Capacity and Dispatch Pro are the reference, but small shops on Reddit dumped auto-dispatch because "technicians would get ready to head to one job and then get reassigned". Suggestions only; the dispatcher drops the ticket.

Drive time, skills, remaining hours. Capacity rule: hold a share of slots for demand calls in season.
F3 · size M

Two-way texting, "on my way" link, confirmations

Table stakes on every platform. Text gets a 45% response rate against 6% for email; the tracking link is the stated cure for "where is my tech" calls.

Twilio with A2P 10DLC registration, which takes 5–15 business days and must start early.
F4 · size M

Caller-ID screen pop and duplicate detection

Standard across ServiceTitan, FieldEdge, Service Fusion, Workiz, Successware and Housecall Pro Voice. Duplicate customers from intake break QuickBooks sync "at least once a week" for Jobber users.

Match on phone and address while the call rings; block the duplicate before it exists.
F5 · size M

Maintenance agreements engine

The recurring-revenue core of every HVAC platform. A 500-agreement book means about 250 visits squeezed into each 6–8 week spring and fall window; monthly billing lifts retention from 65–75% to around 95% in vendor-reported numbers. Fieldline has only a per-site PM price today.

Tiers, assets, generated visits, renewals, card-on-file billing, response-time targets for commercial customers.
F6 · size M

Equipment records from the nameplate

Jobber's lack of equipment records is its most-cited HVAC gap; a FieldEdge shop quit because "it does not track equipment". Nameplate OCR (Bluon, XOi) returns model, age, refrigerant and likely warranty status.

Also the base for the refrigerant log, warranty claims, and the A2L transition records.
F7 · size M

Refrigerant log per job

EPA Section 608 recordkeeping, the January 2026 rule under 40 CFR 84.106 for appliances with 15 lb or more of HFC (commercial refrigeration and comfort cooling; residential and light-commercial AC excluded), and CARB's program for systems over 50 lb all need per-service records of refrigerant added and recovered, leak rate, and technician. Only ServiceTitan and Commusoft ship anything, and neither computes the leak rate.

A differentiator for commercial work, and a compliance requirement either way.
F8 · size M

Checklists and forms

Pass, flag, fail inspection forms are standard; ServiceTitan auto-triggers them on arrival and by equipment type, and ServiceTrade turns flagged items into deficiencies and quotes. Fieldline has no forms.

Required-before-complete is what makes the data reliable.
F9 · size M

Good / Better / Best proposals

Table stakes for sales: ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, FieldEdge, FieldPulse and Workiz all do it. IAQ as the "better versus best" step runs $440–720 per ticket in trade-press figures.

E-sign and a monthly financing figure on the proposal.
F10 · size M

Pricebook

Flat-rate books are standard; the complaint is that techs "have to learn the lingo to find specific repairs". Plain-language names, images, and tiers fix that. Fieldline prices quotes from scratch today.

Supplier price sync (Ferguson, Johnstone) can come later.
F11 · size M

Payments

Card on file, payment links, deposits and tap-to-pay are standard; Fieldline has no payment record at all, so there is no balance due and no aging.

Stripe: SetupIntent for card on file, Terminal or Tap to Pay in the field, Invoicing for links.
F12 · size M

QuickBooks Online sync

"QuickBooks integration creating accounting mess" is a top complaint; the fix is matching on IDs, not names, with a visible alert queue. Every platform syncs to QuickBooks Online.

OAuth2, 60-minute tokens, change-data-capture rather than webhooks.
F13 · size M

Customer portal

Standard on Housecall Pro, Jobber, Workiz, FieldPulse, Simpro and Commusoft; ServiceTitan's lack of one is a noted gap. Jobber users say customers "comment how professional" the hub is.

Approve, pay, history, photos, request service. Commercial property managers want the asset history.
F14 · size S

Review requests and quote follow-up cadences

Review requests on completion are standard and cheap; automated follow-up on unsold quotes lifts close rates by about 25% in reported cases. Fieldline has a manual follow-up date only.

Rules with switches, visible in the follow-up queue, with an opt-out tag.
F15 · size L

Offline-first tech app

The number-one technician complaint across platforms: apps that "freeze mid-job, erase notes, force logouts, or require internet in basements". Even ServiceTitan is only partly offline. Rooftops and mechanical rooms in Orange County have the same problem.

A visible sync state and a retry queue first; a native app with a sync engine later. See the architecture report.
F16 · size M

Truck stock by SKU

Missing parts mean return trips; stock lives in "technician memory, handwritten bin labels". ServiceTitan's min/max replenishment and Commusoft's auto-PO are the reference. Fieldline tracks dollars per category, not parts.

A check of tomorrow's jobs against what is on the van is the piece nobody ships for small shops.
F17 · size M

Photo report and voice-note summary

Housecall Pro, Jobber and Workiz all shipped voice-to-notes and AI job summaries in 2025–26; photo reports with a summary go to the customer. Closeouts get faster and clearer.

On-device dictation, a model to structure it, an editable result.
F18 · size M

Permit and warranty tracker

No platform tracks permits natively. California's 2025 Energy Code took effect January 1, 2026, with HERS now called ECC; SoCalGas rebates require proof of permit closure. Manufacturer warranties need registration within 60–90 days and claims need failure codes, photos and part returns.

Per-job status plus deadlines in the follow-up queue. A real differentiator for a California shop.
F19 · size M

Owner dashboard

"Limited reporting" and "no job costing" are recurring complaints; ServiceTitan's reporting depth is its most-praised feature. Fieldline reports nothing beyond spreadsheet exports.

Booking rate, average ticket, close rate, scorecards, aging, renewals, margin by job type.
F20 · size M

Geofenced timesheets with California overtime

Geofence auto-timers and GPS waypoints at start and stop are standard. California pays daily overtime over 8 hours and double time over 12, with seventh-day rules; Fieldline's meal and rest tracking is already ahead, but its premium detection is switched off in code.

Payroll export to Gusto or QuickBooks.

Left out, and why

  • AI call answering and after-hours booking. Real value (35–45% of HVAC calls arrive after hours), but a large build; integrate a service when the texting layer exists. A missed-call text-back is inside F3 and gets most of the benefit.
  • Route optimization. With four to eight techs in one county, the map plus assignment suggestions capture most of the gain; add a routing API later.
  • Online self-booking. Commercial customers book by phone and email; the portal's "request service" covers it without a public widget.
  • Automatic AI dispatching. Deliberately excluded; small teams report churn when the board reshuffles itself.
  • Weather-driven demand forecasting. Useful later; the capacity view is the prerequisite.
  • Supplier catalog and PO integration. Ferguson and Johnstone integrations are still partial even at ServiceTitan; keep the price-hint tag in purchasing and revisit.
  • Commissions and payroll. Depends on compensation plans not yet decided; the scorecard measures are the input.
  • Fleet telematics and dashcams. Hardware; phone GPS is enough for the pilot.
  • Marketing suite and campaign attribution. Out of the product lane for now.
  • AIA progress billing and retainage. Only matters if install projects become a line of business.

Rules that shaped the compliance features

  • EPA Section 608. Technicians or their employer keep records of refrigerant recovered from appliances with 5–50 lb charges on disposal, and owners of systems over 50 lb keep invoices and leak-inspection records, for three years.
  • 40 CFR 84.106, effective January 1, 2026. For appliances with 15 lb or more of HFC refrigerant (commercial refrigeration and comfort cooling; residential and light-commercial AC and heat pumps excluded), the servicer gives the owner a record per service with pounds added and removed, full charge, leak rate and method, technician and parts; owners keep it three years; leak-rate thresholds trigger repair within 30 days and a follow-up test within 10.
  • CARB refrigerant management. Stationary refrigeration over 50 lb of high-GWP refrigerant: registration and reporting, 14-day leak repair, service and transaction records kept five years.
  • A2L transition. CARB capped new residential AC at 750 GWP from January 2025; the EPA removed its January 2026 federal install deadline in May 2026 for systems whose components were made before 2025, and VRF faces January 2027. Equipment records need per-component manufacture dates and refrigerant type.
  • Title 24, 2025 code. In effect January 1, 2026; HERS verification is now ECC; changeouts need duct leakage, airflow and charge tests registered before final. SoCalGas rebates require proof of permit closure.

Treat the rule summaries as research notes, not legal advice; the sources are in the research folder and should be checked with the company's compliance contact before the features are built.

Architecture · October 14, 2026

Is Fieldline built on the right stack, and is it ready to grow?

Short answer: the languages are right, the framework is used in a way that cancels its benefits, and the data layer has to be replaced before any of the twenty features can exist. The code is a good prototype of the business rules and a poor foundation for a multi-user product. Below: what to keep, change and replace, the target architecture, the risks found in the code, and a phased plan.

What the incumbents run on

From public engineering posts and job listings: ServiceTitan is C#/.NET on Azure with React and a React Native app; Jobber is Ruby on Rails with GraphQL, React and React Native; Housecall Pro is Rails with React and separate native iOS and Android teams; Workiz and BuildOps are Node and TypeScript with React, BuildOps on GraphQL and React Native; FieldPulse is Node with Next.js. None is a no-backend single-page app. Every one has a conventional API over a relational database. Three of six chose React Native for the technician app, and ServiceTitan's path from native to Cordova to React Native is the cautionary tale about shipping wrapped web pages to technicians.

So the answer to "is this industry standard" is: TypeScript and React, yes; a relational database behind an API, yes, and Fieldline does not have one yet; a native or React Native tech app, eventually.

Target architecture

Next.js or Vite: the one decision to make

Option A · Vite + TanStack Router recommended

The app is an authenticated tool with no public pages and no SEO. Vite is the simpler build, TanStack Router gives typed URLs and search params (filters in the URL, which the board and lists need), and nothing about the app wants server rendering. Keep Next, or plain static hosting, for a marketing site if one appears.

Option B · Next.js 16 App Router, no static export

Fine if the team prefers one framework and wants server functions in the same repo. Requires dropping output: "export", the upgrade codemods (middleware becomes proxy, async request APIs), and a Node host. The app stays mostly client-rendered either way.

Risks found in the current code

From a full read of lib/. File and line pointers are in the research notes.

  • Silent total data loss. If the stored document fails a sanity check or does not parse, the app resets to seed data without telling anyone. A key-version bump has no migration path.
  • Hard-coded rates overwrite office data. The journeyman bill rate is forced to a fixed value on every load and every save, so the office cannot change it.
  • Demo data lives in the production load path. Demo customers, sites and work orders are re-merged on every load and cannot be deleted; several "ensure" fixes are specific to the demo company.
  • Photos and PDFs are base64 inside the document. Every action clones and re-serializes all of them; the storage quota is the real capacity limit.
  • Tenant checks are inconsistent. Newer actions filter by organization; older ones (status, assign, update call, notes, time, photos, customer and site updates) look up by id alone. Isolation depends on the UI only listing the current organization.
  • Permission gaps. Setting a work order to billed needs no billing permission; the command parser can set "billed" without an invoice; equipment edits skip the customer-edit permission; a site can be created with no customer.
  • Compliance code is switched off. Missed-meal and rest-premium detection is hard-coded to false, so the premium logic never fires.
  • Two timestamp formats and two rounding regimes. Local stamps without an offset and UTC stamps share fields and are sorted as strings; quarter-hour rounding and two-decimal rounding both feed billing.
  • Weak identifiers. Nine-character random ids and sequence numbers computed from stale state can collide under concurrency.
  • No tests on money and time. Billing, tax, not-to-exceed, credits, timesheets and overtime have no coverage.

Phased plan

  1. Stabilize (1–2 weeks). Tests around billing and timesheet math; add orgId to the six types; remove the rate overwrite and the silent reset; pin Node; start Twilio 10DLC registration and the Stripe and QuickBooks developer accounts, because all three have waiting periods.
  2. Backend (3–4 weeks). Postgres with RLS, Drizzle, versioned migrations, auth with organizations and roles, object storage, a thin API. Port the store's actions to server mutations one feature at a time behind the existing UI. Add routes and URLs.
  3. Redesign build (4–6 weeks). Implement the screens in this pass feature by feature, splitting the monolith as each screen moves: dispatch board first, then work order, customers and equipment, quoting, billing. TanStack Query and the design system land here. Realtime for the board. PWA manifest, service worker and the outbox for techs.
  4. Integrations and new areas (4–6 weeks). Texting and the tracking page, payments, QuickBooks, maps, the agreements engine, pricebook, forms and the refrigerant log, the customer portal, reports.
  5. Native tech app (later). Expo with PowerSync when GPS tracking and true offline are needed in the field. The web tech screens keep working meanwhile.

Running cost for the pilot is small: a managed Postgres and auth tier at about $25 each per month, Twilio and Stripe at usage, maps within free tiers, error tracking free at pilot volume. The expensive part is the engineering in phases 2 and 3, which is why the data layer comes before any new feature.

The README's week-by-week plan already says "real auth, Postgres, photo bucket, replace localStorage" is step two. This review agrees and adds the structure around it: tests first, routes and feature modules during the redesign, integrations after, native last. The README's risk line still holds: if the phone path is slower than a text to the office, stop and fix that before anything else.
